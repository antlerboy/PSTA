Compass v2.0 beta 
Commissioning as collective system learning
Feel free to share via https://link.redquadrant.com/compassv2beta 
‘Commissioning is the public craft of helping a place form and reform the relationships, capabilities, arrangements and conditions through which people can live well, with the system learning from what happens’.
Our original Commissioning Compass
We developed the Commissioning Compass – free at link.redquadrant.com/SmartCompass – not as ‘a better commissioning framework’, but as a disciplined way for people in a place to notice what is happening, question how they are part of it, make sense of the patterns, choose where to act, and learn as the system changes.
The goal was to offer a 'systems assessment for change', not a benchmarking or performance management tool. However, from its origins in adult social care, we found the following traps of language and thinking hard to evade:
The ‘service provision’ default; so much of the work is about getting ‘service provision’ right, the focus can be inwards on ‘the system of provision’ rather than holistic, looking at people living their lives in place.
The ‘controller’ default: since is it so important to think about 'how do we grip demand, manage the market, drive behaviour and deliver outcomes?', it is easy to see the commissioner as outside of the system being analysed, trying to exercise control. This is closely allied with
The ‘colonial’ default, which says: 'how do we engage communities, capture insight, empower people, and roll out our model?', creating an alienating divide between ‘the system’ and people and communities.
And, realistically, with the pressures many commissioners face, the best we can hope for is to ‘assess the eight aspects, pick an approach, implement change.'
Recognising these were all our failings, exacerbated by the very difficult conditions our clients live and work under, we wanted to keep the strengths of the Commissioning Compass but use the eight aspects as lenses in a recursive practice of opening up, sensemaking, strategising, making change, and learning – a subtle but important difference.
That keeps the strength of the Compass – it is meant to help a council, place or partnership see its system more clearly, understand strengths and constraints, and make better choices about where to intervene – but protects it from sliding back into procurement logic, and opens it up as a space- or place-based systems change tool for everyone.
Compass v2.0: the proposition
Commissioning is the public craft of helping a place form and reform the relationships, capabilities, arrangements and conditions through which people can live well, with the system learning from what happens.
Public services start with a need for something to help people achieve their purposes in life, not with commissioning. Commissioning is always separated from the interactions where value is created and from the communities and lives where value contributes. So the Compass should be used from the edge: real demand, first contact, authority to act, what matters to people, and what the system itself is causing.
Systems change as the operating logic
The RedQuadrant systems change model gives the operating logic. Outcomes arise from complex adaptive systems. The levels are not steps. Identity and purpose, seeing the system, intervening, and learning are all active at the same time. Seeing changes what we think our role is. Boundaries shape what we can see. Interventions change what becomes visible. The results of action can question every level.
Therefore the Compass v2.0 is part of a practice of collective system learning. It starts from people living their lives in a place, not from services, budgets or contracts. It opens up the system boundary, makes sense of lived experience and system patterns, chooses strategic moves that fit local conditions, and makes change through relationships, capability, arrangements and forms of help. The eight aspects are not steps or domains. They are lenses for seeing the same living system from different angles. Every use of the Compass should increase shared agency, practical wisdom, and the system’s ability to learn.
Principles
To summarise:
Start with people living well, not services being provided.
Relationships, capability, arrangements and conditions are equally real and important.
Commissioning is a learning practice, not a purchasing cycle; commissioners cannot stand outside the system and move pieces around!
Use the eight aspects (set out below) as lenses in a recursive practice of:
holistic opening up
sensemaking
strategising
making change, and 
collective learning.
That gives the whole model a strong discipline against the defaults we named earlier. Start where life is being lived. Work backwards. Do not start from budgets, structures, services, contracts, or institutional categories and then ask people to fit.
Compass v2.0: the architecture
The model has these axes:
First, the eight aspects. These are the lenses.
Second, the five moves: holistic opening up, sensemaking, strategising, making change. These are the actions. And collective learning at multiple levels is the engine.
RedQuadrant’s five core leadership practices or similar development work are needed to support this kind of model by building honesty, shared sensemaking, productive conversations, clarity, purposeful culture, and good and true intent. link.redquadrant.com/fivecorepractices
The eight aspects stop the work becoming abstract systems talk. The five moves stop the Compass becoming a scoring tool, with the learning engine stopping the whole thing becoming a plan-and-deliver cycle. And the five core practices lay the groundwork.
The system change slides already contain this logic. They connect identity, boundaries, seeing, intervening and learning. They also bring in critical systems, asking whose interests are served, what counts as improvement, who has decision power, what expertise counts, who speaks for those affected but not involved, and what worldview is shaping the work.
That is the direct antidote to provider, controller and colonialist language. The issue is not just vocabulary. It is who gets to define the problem, the system, the evidence, the measure of improvement, and the legitimate action.
The eight aspects as living-system lenses
Whole system conditions
Is this a space that is forming the conditions that generate and support sustainable wellbeing for people, communities and the wider living system?
Not fantasy 'whole system design’; looking at the conditions that make living well easier or harder: housing, care, work, money, transport, ecology, safety, belonging, time, institutions, mutual support, and the histories that shaped the place.
Good relations and patterns of organising
Are the people and organisations involved in good relation and patterns of organising? Can they act, disagree, repair, and learn together?
Not harmony; enough trust, honesty, conflict-capacity and shared purpose to do real work. Do current organising patterns help people act around lived situations?
Growing capability and confidence
Is the capacity, capability, and confidence of people, communities, practitioners, associations, organisations, an economies to help people live well growing? 
Providers, workforce and markets are sometimes categories, but the local ecology of capability includes paid and unpaid care, mutual aid, peer support, civic association, specialist practice, neighbourhood infrastructure, and ordinary human reciprocity.
People, lived purpose
Are people authors of their own purposes, is success judged by whether they can live the life they want? Not users or even citizens – that excludes too many.
Shared sensing, insight, and experiments
Is what matters known to the people who can make a difference? Are curiosity, creativity, practical wisdom, and experimentation alive?
Wider than data: stories; repeated and failure demand; weak signals; silence; conflict and exclusion; workarounds; professional judgement; community knowledge; bright ideas; cost; and the evidence hidden in everyday irritation.
Agency, shared action, and convening change
Are law, policy, money, risk, governance and compliance being interpreted and managed in ways that create legitimate room for people with different stakes and different knowledge to act together without being absorbed.
Shared choosing, reviewing, and adapting action
Are there appropriate rhythms, decision rights, ethical tests, resources, and learning loops for choosing, resourcing, reviewing and adapting action?
Complex systems need more than plans, and more than commissioning. They need living arrangements for deciding, testing, noticing, adapting, stopping, and renegotiating.
Forms of assets, help, infrastructure, and action
Are the forms of assets, help, infrastructure, and action fit for the situation and open to learning?
‘Contracts' and 'delivery models’ are possible forms, not the point. Civic associations, neighbourhood practices, shared data, grants, social infrastructure investments, funding, partnerships, alliances, direct payments, family group processes, or simply stopping the system from making life harder also count.
The five moves
Holistic opening up
This is the boundary move.
It asks: what system are we talking about, who is included, who is missing, who is affected but not involved, what histories are active, what future costs are being displaced, what forms of knowledge are ignored, and what would change if the boundary moved?
It should come before sensemaking, but it also returns throughout. Every time the system changes, the boundary changes. Every time a new voice enters, the problem changes.
This is where critical systems thinking belongs. It stops the model from pretending that 'the system' is an innocent object waiting to be mapped. Systems are partly made by the boundaries we draw and the purposes we privilege. The system change slides are clear that definitions, perspectives, boundaries and context are sources of power, and that shared learning is how the system begins to learn about itself.
Sensemaking
This is the pattern move.
It asks: what is happening, what keeps happening, what is working, what is being carried by goodwill, what demand is real, what demand is created by the system, where are people being passed around, where is authority missing, where are relationships doing hidden labour, and what does the system know but not yet admit?
This is where the Compass scores can help, but only as prompts. The company summary explicitly says that the discussion is often more valuable than the number, and that differences in scoring reveal how people experience the system.
Sensemaking should combine three kinds of evidence: lived experience, operational evidence from the edge, and system evidence about flows, rules, money, capacity and consequences. Any one of these on its own will lie.
Strategising
This is the choice move.
It asks: given what we now see, where should attention, authority, money, relationship and learning go next?
Strategy here is not a grand plan. It is a set of explicit wagers. 'If we change this condition, with these people, in this part of the system, we expect these patterns to shift, and we will know by looking here.'
The Compass helps because it refuses the idea that there is one best commissioning model. The company summary says the right approach depends on local conditions, history, relationships, capability, community strengths, markets, policy pressures and room for manoeuvre. It also warns that ambition must be matched with foundations.
This matters. A place may want whole-system wellbeing, but if information is poor, relationships are brittle, legal confidence is low, decision rights are unclear, or the practical forms of help are wrong, the strategy has to start there. Otherwise the aspiration becomes theatre.
Making change
This is the intervention move.
It asks: what can we now change in conversations, measures, rules, resource flows, roles, authority, agreements, data, routines, infrastructure and forms of help?
The phrase 'making change' is better than 'delivering change'. Delivery language implies a centre, a product, a recipient and compliance. Making change implies participation in a living situation.
The work may include experiments, but not pilots in the usual doomed sense. A useful test should alter a real condition and teach the system something. It should not be a decorative innovation beside business as usual.
The workbook material supports this developmental rhythm. It asks people to score, gather evidence, sense-check with others in the system, explore constraints and enablers, test intervention points, and use later workshops to explore approaches and improvements.
Collective learning
Learning must not be treated as ‘just move five’ – it is the engine of the whole model.
We use single, double and triple loop learning: learning about whether actions work, learning about how the system works, and learning about identity, purpose and boundaries. We also distinguish learning by 'us' as system changers and learning by the place-based system itself. ‘Collective learning’ is not evaluation after delivery – it is the system noticing what action has revealed about itself.
It asks whether people, practitioners, communities, partners and institutions have become more able to see, decide and act together. It includes single-loop learning about whether the action worked, double-loop learning about the assumptions behind the action, and triple-loop learning about identity, purpose and power.
The killer question is: ‘What has this changed about who knows, who decides, who acts, and who learns?’
For the integrated model, use four learning questions after any significant action.
What changed in people’s lived experience?
What did we learn about how the system behaves?
What did we learn about our arrangements, rules, measures, relationships and forms of help?
What did this reveal about who we think we are, what we think we are here for, and whose purposes count?
The last question is the one most frameworks dodge. It is also the one that stops system change becoming paternalistic improvement work.
Discipline on language and framing
The main risk is not one bad phrase. It is a whole grammar.
Provider drift says: 'How do we get the right providers delivering the right services?'
Controller drift says: 'How do we grip demand, manage the market, drive behaviour and deliver outcomes?'
Colonial drift says: 'How do we engage communities, capture insight, empower people, and roll out our model?'
The integrated model needs a different grammar.
Use 'people living their lives', not 'service users'.
Use 'forms of help', not 'service provision', except where the legal or technical distinction matters.
Use 'people and organisations who offer help', not 'providers', unless discussing formal contractual roles.
Use 'make visible', not 'capture insight'.
Use 'shared sensing', not 'data extraction'.
Use 'decision rights', not 'engagement'.
Use 'room for agency', not 'permission from the centre'.
Use 'altering conditions', not 'fixing people'.
Use 'learning from the edge', not 'consulting the frontline'.
Use 'people with different stakes and knowledge', not 'stakeholders' when the humanity matters.
Use 'making change' or ’intervening’, not 'delivering change'.
Use 'fit for the situation', not 'best practice'.
Of course, the real point isn’t to police language. Our papers on relational public services https://chosen-path.org/2026/03/21/collection-of-posts-on-relational-public-services/, especially the ‘ladder’ https://chosen-path.org/2026/03/17/what-do-we-mean-when-we-say-relational-in-public-services/, give a sharper test. Lower levels are organised around system comfort and control. Higher levels are organised around people’s lived purpose and shared power, with the state learning to be less of a machine and more of an enabling infrastructure. That’s the kind of guardrail we need to be putting in for ourselves.
Power
Never forget to look at explicit decision rights. The Compass already has strong questions about people being in charge of their own outcomes, and the relationality ladder names co-governed place and citizen space. But if people define outcomes but institutions keep the real decisions, the language improves while the power relation stays the same. So the standing test across all eight aspects is: 'who can define, decide, resource, challenge, stop, and learn?' That one question will expose most provider, controller, and colonial drift before it hardens into your model.
