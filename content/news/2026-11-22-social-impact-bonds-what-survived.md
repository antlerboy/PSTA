Social impact bonds were meant to help public bodies fund services by paying for verified results, with outside investors taking some of the financial risk. They attracted attention well beyond the small number of contracts that were eventually signed.

In [*Whatever Happened to Social Impact Bonds?*](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/social-impact-bonds-failure-to-scale), published online on 16 November 2026 for the *Stanford Social Innovation Review* Winter 2027 issue, **Tracy Palandjian and Jake Segal of Social Finance** describe what happened to the idea they helped develop.

Their account is candid about the obstacles. Each deal demanded bespoke negotiation, intensive work on measurement, agreement over risk, and sustained government capacity. A programme could be delivering worthwhile support while the financing arrangement around it became too complicated to maintain. The authors argue that the broader work of measuring and paying attention to outcomes has continued even as the original financing model lost momentum.

Their experience is valuable evidence from practitioners closely involved in those deals; it isn't an independent finding that all outcomes-based contracts succeed or fail.

For commissioners, the questions are more useful than the label. What outcome is actually uncertain? Would linking payment to it change a decision or a service response? Who bears the risk when referrals, data, or people's circumstances differ from the model? What can be learned without building a new financial vehicle?
