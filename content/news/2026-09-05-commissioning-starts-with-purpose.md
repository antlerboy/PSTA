A commissioning conversation often begins with the service being purchased. That makes sense administratively, but it can conceal the reason for buying it. Start one step earlier: what should someone be able to do, experience, or achieve, and what currently makes that difficult?

For a person arranging support at home, the important outcome might be being able to get up, see friends, and feel secure. A specification expressed only in visits and minutes gives a much narrower account. Both resource and operational detail matter; the task is to keep them connected to the person’s purpose.

Try a short exercise with colleagues from commissioning, delivery, finance, and the community. Follow one real journey. Identify the decisions that helped, the work that had to be repeated, and the things nobody had authority to resolve. Separate evidence from assumptions. Then choose one commissioning decision that could change the conditions you have found.

The useful result is a question you can investigate together, with someone responsible for the next step. Review whether the change helps people and what it costs elsewhere in the system.
